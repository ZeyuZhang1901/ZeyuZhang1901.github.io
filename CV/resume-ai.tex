\section{Summary}
I am a fourth-year Ph.D.\ student in Statistics and Data Science at Northwestern University (GPA 3.95/4.00; expected graduation June 2028). My main research interest is \textbf{LLM forecasting}, and my recent research asks how to make \textbf{LLM backtests reliable}. A central and hard obstacle is \textbf{temporal leakage (look-ahead bias)}: knowledge from pretraining that lets a model ``read the future'' during a backtest. I also study the robustness of LLM prediction systems: how they are affected by attacks. In addition, I am interested in the \textbf{interpretation} of LLM predictions---whether the prediction a model states reflects its internal reasoning and knowledge. My strengths are \textbf{LLM evaluation and large-scale experiments} on frontier models, \textbf{post-training LLMs} with reinforcement learning, and a solid \textbf{statistics} foundation.

\section{Research Experience}

\entry{Trustworthy LLM Backtesting: Temporal Leakage and Look-Ahead Bias}
  {Northwestern University}
  {Advisor: Prof.\ Bradly C.\ Stadie}{Sept 2025 -- Nov 2026}
\story{Backtesting evaluates a model's prediction ability by asking historical questions as if it were forecasting from
  the earlier time. Its validity breaks when applying to LLMs: post-cutoff knowledge from pretraining leaks into the
  model's reasoning and inflates the backtest score.}
\begin{bullets}
  \item \facet{Theory and adjusted scores.} Built a mathematical model of temporal leakage in LLM backtests.
        LLMs legitimately know more about times near their cutoff, so recency mimics leakage: proved no
        passive backtest can separate recency and leakage from genuine skill, and showed it on four flagship
        models. Leakage concentrates on hard questions, where the honest forecast is far from the outcome,
        and even partial memorization captures most of the inflation (the quadratic ``double benefit,''
        $w(2-w)$). Introduced two estimators---a known cutoff (regression discontinuity) and a clean control
        model (difference-in-differences)---that yield a \textbf{leakage-adjusted score}; in twin models with
        planted leakage, they tracked the dose and returned null on clean questions.
  \item \facet{Interpretable audit and inference-time defense.} Designed \textbf{Shapley-DCLR}, an
        interpretable metric of what fraction of a model's decision-driving reasoning is contaminated, and
        \textbf{TimeSPEC}, an architecture that interleaves temporally filtered retrieval with claim-level
        supervision so predictions rest entirely on pre-cutoff evidence. It reports backtest performance
        honestly---harder tasks such as stock-return ranking typically show a drop---and cut leakage by
        \textbf{83--89\%} versus unsupervised retrieval-augmented prediction across three LLM families (Qwen3.5, Kimi-K2.5, Claude Sonnet 4).
  \item \facet{Training leakage out.} Designed \textbf{TEMPO}, GRPO + LoRA post-training of Qwen3 MoE models (30B/35B) with a \textbf{two-mode
        reward}: a leakage mode drives post-cutoff claims to zero as a hard prerequisite before a performance
        mode optimizes the task on clean evidence, letting the model discover temporally valid reasoning;
        proved monotonic leakage descent and convergence to the leak-free optimum. Across three tasks and two
        models, leakage fell from \textbf{2--13\% to 0.6--3.7\%}; performance rose 6--13\% where pre-cutoff
        signal is strong and held where the task is hard.
\end{bullets}

\entry{One Fabrication Is Enough to Move LLM Forecasts}
  {Northwestern University}
  {Advisor: Prof.\ Bradly C.\ Stadie}{Aug 2026 -- Nov 2026}
\story{Retrieval-based LLM forecasters search the news for evidence. We study the weakest attacker this
  allows: one who knows only the forecasting question and publishes one fabricated article, with no access
  to the model, retriever, or queries.}
\begin{bullets}
  \item \facet{The attack reaches the systems in use.} Evaluated the attack against five frontier models and
        the architectures of two leading deployed forecasting systems, on real stock and prediction-market
        questions (ForecastBench, Polymarket, Kalshi). Once in context, one fabricated article reverses
        \textbf{74--82\%} of opposing forecasts, and the deployed systems' search retrieves it in
        \textbf{$\sim$90\%} of runs.
  \item \facet{Why it works.} Showed that retrieval decides the outcome: once the article is retrieved, every
        model follows it. A genuine article in the same position has one third of the effect, so the effect
        comes from the fabrication, not the added document.
  \item \facet{A second attack.} Designed the first destabilizing attack on a forecaster: two fabricated
        articles arguing opposite directions. The model does not hedge; it returns a confident forecast in a
        direction the attacker no longer controls.
  \item \facet{Against the best available defenses.} Passage filtering and partitioned retrieval each stop
        one article but are cheaply bypassed; combined, they stop both bypasses at four articles but are beaten
        by sixteen, change 29\% of clean decisions, and require control of the retrieval index, which
        open-web systems do not have.
\end{bullets}
